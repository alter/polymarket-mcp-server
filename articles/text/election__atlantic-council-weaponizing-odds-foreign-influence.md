# Weaponizing the Odds: Prediction Markets as a New Vector for Foreign Influence

**Author:** Matthew Wein  
**Source:** Atlantic Council  
**Date:** February 2026

## Core Thesis

Prediction market platforms — increasingly cited by mainstream media as authoritative signals — present significant national security risks by enabling foreign adversaries to manipulate both financial markets and public narratives simultaneously.

## Main Arguments

### Market Vulnerability
Prediction markets "present themselves as neutral aggregators" but are "often thin, sometimes dominated by a relatively small pool of sophisticated bettors." This concentration creates opportunities for coordinated manipulation.

On many platforms, even the largest markets feature extremely low volume where a single small bet of a few thousand dollars can immediately move the market by several percentage points in real time.

### Narrative Weaponization
Actors could rationally allocate resources to move market prices upward, then "cite that movement to entertainment reporters as evidence of strong anticipated performance." This creates self-reinforcing false signals.

When odds on an imminent strike or an election outcome move sharply and media outlets broadcast that movement as the informed market consensus, that reported signal starts influencing how journalists frame events, what the public sees as likely and legitimate, and even how adversaries perceive intent.

### Escalation to National Security
Foreign intelligence services possessing classified information could simultaneously profit from and weaponize that data by taking market positions while "amplifying the resulting price movements as evidence of impending events."

A state actor wanting to manipulate crisis dynamics could move a thinly traded geopolitical market for a few million dollars — plausibly deniable and far cheaper than mobilizing military assets — and manufacture the appearance of insider knowledge about imminent action.

### Erosion of Trust
Repeated allegations of market manipulation — particularly in sports — can "spill over into attitudes toward other large-scale events and institutions, including elections, financial markets, and government processes."

## Historical Examples

- 2024 US Presidential Election: French whale's $30M+ Trump bets raised manipulation fears
- Suspicious trades preceding Venezuelan President Maduro operation (January 2026)
- Suspicious Polymarket activity related to Israeli military operations
- Hungarian election: traders not local voters shaping odds based on Western media narratives
- Irish Dublin Central by-election: Irish government examined suspicious $1M bets

## Policy Recommendations

The author proposes four lines of effort:

1. Media organizations should adopt "transparent standards for when and how prediction market data are cited," emphasizing limited participant pools
2. Regulators should implement "disclosure requirements for large or coordinated positions" in sensitive markets
3. Platforms must develop "robust market integrity monitoring" detecting anomalous trading patterns
4. Security authorities should incorporate prediction markets into "broader threat assessments of the information environment"

## Broader Context

Iranian intelligence was almost certainly monitoring Polymarket before the February [2026] strikes. The credibility of prediction markets as predictive tools could earn them a higher rank in the information landscape available to voters, with endogeneity between market prices and behavior difficult to rule out.
