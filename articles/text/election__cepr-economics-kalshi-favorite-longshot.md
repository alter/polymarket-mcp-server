# The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market: Favorite-Longshot Bias

**Source:** CEPR VoxEU  
**Date:** 2025

## Overview

An analysis of Kalshi prediction market data examining calibration, the favorite-longshot bias, and market efficiency in political and other event contracts.

## Key Findings

Analysis of over **300,000 contracts** and their outcomes demonstrates that Kalshi's contract prices are informative and improve in accuracy as markets approach closing, but they display a clear **favourite-longshot bias**.

**Favorite-Longshot Bias Definition:**
Low-price contracts win far less often than required to break even, while high-price contracts win more often and yield small positive returns.

In plain terms:
- A 10-cent contract will win less often than 10% of the time (overpriced underdogs)
- An 85-cent contract will win more often than 85% of the time (underpriced favorites)

## Political Market Specifics

Political contracts display "persistent underconfidence" — prices are systematically compressed toward 50%. A 70-cent political contract may actually represent an 83% probability in the week before resolution.

This systematic bias in political markets reflects:
1. Opposing partisan bettors who both overweight their candidate's chances
2. The compression effect as motivated reasoning cancels out rather than aggregates

## Implications for Election Forecasting

The favorite-longshot bias means that when interpreting prediction market prices for elections:
- Don't take prices at face value — recalibration is needed
- A candidate trading at 60% may actually have a 65–70% chance of winning
- A candidate trading at 30% may actually have only a 20–25% chance

This systematic distortion means prediction market prices require domain-specific recalibration to be used as accurate probability estimates — particularly for political events.

## Arbitrage Opportunities

The study found arbitrage opportunities peaked in the final two weeks before the 2024 presidential election, suggesting market efficiency broke down precisely when it was most needed. This is consistent with the Vanderbilt finding that daily price changes were weakly correlated or negatively autocorrelated.
