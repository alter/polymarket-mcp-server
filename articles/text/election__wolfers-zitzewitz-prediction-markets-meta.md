# Prediction Markets and Election Accuracy: Key Historical Academic Literature

## Overview

This document synthesizes the key historical academic literature on prediction market accuracy in elections, centered on Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2004) and related studies.

## Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2004) — Foundational Review

**Citation:** Wolfers, J. & Zitzewitz, E. (2004). "Prediction Markets." *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 18(2): 107–126.

The foundational paper establishing that prediction markets are generally more accurate than polling as forecasting mechanisms. Using prediction market prices has yielded "impressively accurate predictions for a wide array of outcomes" — typically exceeding the accuracy of "just asking" methods such as opinion polls or expert forecasts.

The paper cited accuracy in political markets as well as markets for printer sales, macro-economic statistics, and Hollywood box office performance.

## IEM Historical Track Record

- **Average absolute forecast error in US presidential races:** 1.13 percentage points
- **Comparison to 964 polls** across five presidential elections (1988–2004): IEM outperformed polls 74% of the time
- **100+ days before election:** IEM "significantly outperforms the polls in every election"

## Erikson & Wlezien (2012) — Historical Assessment

**Citation:** Erikson, R.S. & Wlezien, C. (2012). "Markets vs. polls as election predictors: An historical assessment." *Electoral Studies*.

**Key finding:** Market prices are far better predictors in the period *without* polls than when polls were available. Market prices of the pre-poll era predicted elections almost on par with polls following the introduction of scientific polling in the 1930s. When both market prices and polls are available, prices add "nothing" to election prediction beyond what polls already offer.

This challenges the narrative that prediction markets provide independent information beyond polls — they may largely be tracking poll data.

## Berg, Nelson & Rietz (2008)

Demonstrated that IEM vote-share market forecasts are closer to eventual outcomes than polls 74% of the time overall, with accuracy increasing relative to polls further in advance of the election.

## Leigh & Wolfers (2006), Rothschild (2009), Williams & Reade (2016)

Multiple studies continuously attested prediction market accuracy superior to poll-based forecasts across different elections and contexts.

## Favorite-Longshot Bias Literature

**Page & Clemen:** Found evidence for a favourite-longshot bias — predictions are better when the predicted event is close in time. For events more than a year away, prices are biased towards 50% — likely due to traders' time preferences.

## Wlezien (2008): Long-Shot Bias

Documented cases of long-shot bias in winner-take-all US elections, where markets overvalued underdogs.

## Key Caveat

IEM traders are self-selected and differ greatly from a representative sample of voters, which limits the "wisdom of crowds" interpretation. The market's accuracy may reflect sophisticated information aggregation by a small informed minority rather than genuine democratic aggregation.
