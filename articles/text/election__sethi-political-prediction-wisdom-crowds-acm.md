# Political Prediction and the Wisdom of Crowds

**Authors:** Rajiv Sethi (Barnard College, Columbia University), Julie Seager (MIT), Fred Morstatter, Daniel M. Benjamin, Anna Hammell, Tianshuo Liu, Sachi Patel, Ramya Subramanian  
**Published:** ACM Collective Intelligence Conference (CI '25), June 2025  
**Also available:** SSRN preprint (abstract_id=5296936)

## Abstract

This study evaluates the relative forecasting performance of three statistical models and a prediction market for several outcomes decided during the November 2024 U.S. elections — the winner of the presidency, the popular vote, fifteen competitive states in the Electoral College, eleven Senate races, and thirteen House races.

## Key Findings

**Presidential Race (High-Visibility):**
All three statistical models (The Economist, Silver Bulletin, FiveThirtyEight) failed to beat the prediction market for the headline presidential contract, where many informed people actively bet.

**Down-Ballot Races (Low-Visibility):**
Prediction markets fared "particularly poorly down the ballot compared to the models, especially for Congressional races." The researchers attribute this to lower trading volumes in those markets.

**Some statistical models did beat the market** convincingly in contracts referencing less visible races.

**Overall:** When averaging across a broader range of electoral outcomes (competitive states and congressional races), all three models and the market achieved significantly better performance than a naive guess.

**Coin Flip Baseline:** For the specific question of predicting the winner of the presidency and popular vote, a coin flip would have produced a more accurate forecast (since both candidates were close to 50-50 in formal model terms).

## Novel Evaluation Methodology

The researchers critique conventional Brier score approaches, which can reward modeling flaws that result in predictable reversals. Instead, they adopt a test based on the idea that "the strength of a model can be measured by the profitability of a trader who believes its forecasts and bets on the market based on this belief."

## Expert Commentary

Rajiv Sethi: "My view of prediction markets is that they have the potential to generate valuable forecasts for certain events, especially when we're in uncharted waters and history is not a good guide to the future."

Sethi stresses that the apparent success of prediction markets on the Trump-Harris election, "while notable, is merely a single data point, and they did not correctly forecast other election outcomes. One needs to look at a larger range of events — when you do that, the jury is still out on the accuracy of markets versus models."

## Context

This paper directly addresses the specific claim that Polymarket's 2024 success demonstrated superiority over polling and models. The careful methodology reveals the comparison is more nuanced than headline narratives suggest: markets excel on high-profile binary outcomes; statistical models excel on down-ballot races where market liquidity is thin.
