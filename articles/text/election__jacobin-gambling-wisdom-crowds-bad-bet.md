# Gambling on the Wisdom of Crowds Is a Bad Bet

**Author:** Rafferty Thompson  
**Source:** Jacobin  
**Date:** February 2026

## Overview

A leftist critique of prediction market platforms (PPMs) and the "wisdom of crowds" argument used to justify their expansion. Thompson argues that PPMs generate prices based on speculation and market design rather than reliable forecasting ability.

## The Wisdom of Crowds Thesis Deconstructed

The concept originates from statistician Francis Galton's 1907 observation that eight hundred people guessing an ox's weight had a median estimate only nine pounds off actual weight. However, Thompson notes this required expert intervention: "Galton collected and tabulated the guesses, calculated the average, and excluded thirteen guesses because their handwriting was illegible."

The "wisdom of crowds" effect depends on carefully designed conditions — diversity of information, independence of judgment, and proper aggregation mechanisms. These conditions are systematically violated in political prediction markets.

## Market Infrastructure Problems

PPMs depend on carefully designed market structures, not inherent crowd intelligence. Nobel laureate Vernon L. Smith's experimental economics research argues that "rationality only emerges under carefully constructed market structures."

Kalshi cofounder Tarek Mansour revealed the industry's true goal: to "monetize any difference of opinion" and "financialize everything." Yet economic research demonstrates prediction markets won't create accurate forecasts universally.

## Verification Problem for Non-Repeatable Events

A critical flaw emerges with non-repeatable events. When Polymarket refused payouts on Venezuela betting, disputes arose over defining "invasion." Markets on singular events lack independent verification standards, making probabilities "little more than a number attached to a price."

Without benchmarks — unlike elections where results are measurable — prices become meaningless speculation rather than wisdom.

## Political Market Specifics

Thompson argues that political prediction markets attract motivated reasoners, not dispassionate probability assessors. The demographic skew toward young, male, crypto-native participants creates systematic bias rather than the diverse, independent crowd the wisdom-of-crowds theory requires.

The concentrate ownership problem: blockchain data shows "roughly 70% of Polymarket traders lose money while fewer than 0.04% accounts have captured more than 70% of all realized gains." This is not a wisdom-of-crowds outcome — it's a concentrated information advantage.

## Conclusion

Prediction markets represent the financialization of epistemic uncertainty. Rather than democratizing forecasting, they create new mechanisms for well-positioned insiders to extract rents from uninformed participants while being held up as oracles of public truth. The "wisdom of crowds" framing is ideological cover for a gambling mechanism that systematically transfers wealth from many to few.
