# Historical Political Futures Markets: 1880s–1930s New York

**Authors:** Paul Rhode and Koleman Strumpf  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w14377  
**Date:** October 2008

## Overview

Research on historical political betting markets in the United States, documenting the large-scale, organized prediction market activity in New York from the 1880s through the 1930s — markets that prefigured modern platforms like Polymarket by over a century.

## Scale and Volume

From the 1880s to the 1930s, New York City was the hub of political wagering, which sometimes exceeded the stock market in daily volume. Activity shifted from poolrooms to the Curb Exchange (precursor to AMEX) and Wall Street offices.

**The 1916 election** was the high point, with some **$165 million** (in 2002 dollars) wagered in organized New York markets — more than twice the total spending on election campaigns that year.

**Average betting turnover** per U.S. presidential election is estimated at over **50% of campaign spend**.

## How It Worked

Before World War II, election betting was widespread dating back to George Washington's election and becoming organized by Lincoln's era. Though often illegal, it operated openly through "betting commissioners" who held stakes and charged a 5% commission.

Newspapers regularly reported the odds, and observers treated them as serious indicators of likely election outcomes. Reporting on bets ahead of the 1924 presidential election, *The New York Times* observed: "It is an old axiom in the financial district that Wall Street betting odds are 'never wrong.'"

## Historical Accuracy

Economic historians have shown these markets were often surprisingly accurate — in several elections they matched or even outperformed contemporary political forecasts.

## Decline

The heyday extended from the 1890s through the mid-1910s. Key factors in the decline:
- **Hart-Agnew Act (1908/1910):** New York state legislation outlawed professional bookmaking
- Changes in tax laws
- Changing public attitudes towards organized financial markets
- Rise of scientific polling (George Gallup, 1930s)
- Increasing availability of other forms of gambling

Political betting markets ultimately disappeared largely because of the rise of scientific polls and other gambling alternatives.

## Relevance to Modern Markets

Rhode and Strumpf's research demonstrates that prediction markets for elections are not a novel innovation but a return to a long historical tradition. The accuracy debates, manipulation concerns, and regulatory conflicts of 2024 closely mirror controversies from a century earlier.
