# Forecasting Elections: Comparing Prediction Markets, Polls, and Their Biases

**Source:** ResearchGate / Published in Electoral Studies  
**Period covered:** 1988–2004 US Presidential Elections

## Overview

A systematic comparison of prediction market accuracy against traditional polling for US presidential elections, examining both systematic biases and relative performance.

## Key Findings

**Overall Performance:** Prediction markets outperform polls approximately 74% of the time when both are available. The advantage is largest more than 100 days before the election.

**Temporal Advantage:** Prediction markets' advantage over polls is greatest far from election day, when polls are sparse and market prices incorporate more diverse information.

**Convergence Near Election Day:** As election day approaches and more polls become available, the gap between market accuracy and poll accuracy narrows significantly.

## Biases Identified

**In Prediction Markets:**
- **Favorite-longshot bias:** Systematic overvaluation of longshot candidates
- **Partisan bias:** Traders who support a candidate may bet more than is justified
- **Herding:** Prices can get "stuck" on focal points (50%, 75%, 90%) due to cognitive anchoring

**In Polls:**
- **Sampling error:** Particularly large for rare events or small demographic groups
- **Likely voter models:** Can misestimate turnout
- **House effects:** Systematic partisan lean in polling firms
- **Timing effects:** Polls capture opinion at a moment, not a probability

## Combined Approach

The paper suggests that combining market prices with polling data and fundamentals-based models produces superior forecasts. Neither markets nor polls alone dominate, and each provides distinct information.

## Historical Context

Covers 5 US presidential elections: 1988, 1992, 1996, 2000, 2004. The Iowa Electronic Markets were the primary market studied, with investment caps of $500 per person.

The small-scale, academically-operated IEM is very different from modern Polymarket, but established the foundational evidence that markets can aggregate political information efficiently.
