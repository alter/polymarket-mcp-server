# The Case Against Political Prediction Markets

**Author:** Ian Bremmer (Eurasia Group)  
**Source:** GZERO Media

## Overview

Ian Bremmer argues that political prediction markets, despite theoretical benefits, impose greater societal costs than benefits due to systematic insider trading, corruption incentives, and threats to democratic governance.

## Key Evidence of Misconduct

**Insider Trading Cases:**
- Before the February 28, 2026 US-Israel strike on Iran, over 150 accounts on Polymarket correctly predicted the attack's specific date
- Account "magamyman" turned $87,000 into $553,000 by trading 71 minutes before news broke
- Another user netted $2.14 million across multiple Iran-related contracts

**Statistical Findings:**
A Harvard study examined "93,000 markets and nearly 50,000 unique wallets" and found suspicious trading pairs achieving "69.9% win rate" with "$143 million in anomalous profits over two years."

Blockchain data shows "roughly 70% of Polymarket traders lose money while fewer than 0.04% accounts have captured more than 70% of all realized gains."

## Core Arguments Against These Markets

**The Corruption Problem:**
When individuals can profit from outcomes they influence — military commanders, diplomats, intelligence analysts, elected officials — prediction markets create "systematic incentives to corrupt" truth-seeking rather than facilitate it.

**Structural Issues:**
- Zero-sum nature means insider profits directly extract from retail participants
- Anonymity and blockchain infrastructure make accountability impossible
- Creates "mechanism for the politically connected to convert their access into private profit"

**Democratic Governance Threat:**
The markets "financialize the erosion" of institutional trust required for democracy. Citizens with financial positions become "bettors with a directional position" rather than engaged citizens evaluating governance.

**Addiction and Financial Harm:**
Studies show legalized mobile sports betting since 2018 "increased personal bankruptcy rates by around 10%" in affected states, "tripled calls to gambling helplines," and left "roughly 1 in 5 young men under 25 on the spectrum of a gambling disorder."

## Legitimate Use Cases (Limited)

Bremmer acknowledges prediction markets perform adequately for:
- Apolitical technical questions (inflation forecasts, Fed rate decisions)
- Hedging risks for corporations exposed to specific geopolitical events

However, these require conditions — "independent participants, dispersed information, and no ability to move the outcome" — that political markets systematically violate.

## National Security Concerns

Movement in geopolitical market odds influences how journalists frame events, shapes public perception of legitimacy, and signals intent to adversaries. State actors could "move a thinly traded geopolitical market for a few million dollars" to manufacture false consensus about imminent military action.

## Regulatory Response

Recent governance efforts include:
- California Governor Newsom's executive order banning state officials from using nonpublic information
- Bipartisan congressional bills restricting trading by federal officials
- Platform announcements banning stolen information trades

However, Bremmer notes CEO statements describing insider trading as "sort of an inevitability" reveal "the limits of self-policing."

## Eurasia Group's Response

Two weeks before publication, Eurasia Group banned all staff and families from prediction market betting — "not due to specific incidents but because 'the structural incentive is there regardless of individual ethics' when analysts possess nonpublic information about tradable events."

## Conclusion

Bremmer frames prediction markets as emblematic of America's "rotten political economy," functioning as wealth redistribution mechanisms from desperate retail participants to informed insiders, deepening "the desperation and mistrust that drives people into the casino."
