# Only 3% of Traders Drive Prediction Markets' Accuracy, Not the Crowd, Study Finds

**Source:** CoinDesk  
**Date:** April 26, 2026

## Key Study

**Paper:** "Prediction Market Accuracy: Crowd Wisdom or Informed Minority?"  
**Authors:** Roberto Gomez-Cram, Yunhan Guo, Howard Kung (London Business School) & Theis Ingerslev Jensen (Yale)  
**Published:** April 20, 2026 on SSRN

## Core Finding

The researchers analyzed the complete transaction history on Polymarket, covering 98,906 events, 210,322 markets, and $13.76 billion in total trading volume across 1.72 million accounts. The paper finds that about **3% of traders account for most price discovery**, undermining the idea that prediction markets rely on the wisdom of the crowd.

## Methodology

To distinguish skill from luck, researchers "reran each trader's bets 10,000 times, keeping everything the same except the direction," using coin flips to establish a baseline. This identified which traders genuinely outperformed random chance.

## Critical Statistics

- Only **12% of the biggest winners** by raw profit cleared the skill benchmark
- Roughly **60% of "lucky winners"** became losers when tested against separate events
- The remaining 97% of traders primarily provide liquidity but collectively lose to the informed minority

## Skill Persistence

Skilled Polymarket traders retained their classification **44% of the time out-of-sample**, compared to just 10% for skilled mutual funds — suggesting more robust and persistent skill in prediction markets.

## Price Discovery Impact

Skilled traders consistently predict outcomes correctly and move prices toward accurate resolutions. They react first to new information like Federal Reserve announcements and corporate earnings, while other traders show minimal consistent response.

## Insider Trading Evidence

Three newly created accounts profited over $630,000 by betting on Nicolás Maduro's removal days before the operation, with odds then at roughly 10%. The accounts were later linked to military personnel with advance knowledge.

## Implications

The finding directly challenges the "wisdom of crowds" narrative used to justify prediction markets' epistemic authority. Rather than aggregating dispersed public knowledge, market accuracy is driven by a tiny informed elite — which raises questions about whether the broader public should treat prediction market prices as democratic information signals.
